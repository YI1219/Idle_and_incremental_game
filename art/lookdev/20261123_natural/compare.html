Left of the waterfall, a joint in the middle terrace wall read as a hollow cavity. 2. Slopes and ramps

You asked for slopes and ramps to be buildable. The island format now lets a cell carry a slope, and a ramp stroke joins two terraces; the generator then joins the surfaces, makes a soft grassy bank for small rises and a wedge of cut rock for large ones. Ramps work in any direction, including diagonals.

The ramp stroke rule. A ramp is drawn from a point on one terrace to a point on another, with a width; every cell whose centre lies inside that strip becomes part of one inclined plane running from the first height to the second. It is refused if either end is water or off the island, if it is shorter than 2 m, steeper than 0.6 (about 31 degrees), joins two equal heights, or would cover water.

Known differences and limits

  • Rock at native pixels: the painted brushwork on the cliff faces is blotchier and less vertically streaked than Godot's, and the tops of the buttress slabs show a faint crease.
  • Ramps read gently in the general views because grass covers them like any lawn; that is why each has its own camera view.
  • A ramp is a flat plane per cell with rounded creases between cells, not yet a free-form hill tool.
  • The 4,096-cell island with real props is close to the 12 ms budget while moving (an estimated 10 to 11 ms on an idle machine; 13.4 to 14.2 ms as measured on the busy machine, over the budget): cut-out leaf cards are drawn over each other many times and props have no simpler distance versions yet.
  • The three-island wallpaper view still uses a simpler cloud sea and no far hills.
  • Godot's far depth-of-field blur is not ported; distant things are paler, not blurred.
  • No sparkle glints on the water.
  • Nothing moves in these stills: wind, water and the falls are built to move and are shown frozen.
  • All of this is study-tool code, to be rebuilt in the real renderer.
